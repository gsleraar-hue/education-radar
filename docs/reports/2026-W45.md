# Education Radar - week 45, 2026-11-02

## Week in one glance
The Dutch debate about screens and AI in class moved from talk to Parliament: a majority of the Tweede Kamer backs a ban on generative AI for primary pupils, while an RTL Nieuws survey found schools at every level cutting back on laptops. Flanders set out tougher, subject-based minimum goals for the first stage of secondary school, with Dutch and maths as hard requirements. In England, the government paid £5.8bn to clear most council SEND deficits, but the question of who pays from 2026-27 onwards remains open.

## Top 3

### 1. Dutch Parliament majority backs a ban on generative AI for primary pupils (Netherlands, K-12)
**What happened.** According to Computable (30 Oct), a motion by Laurens Dassen (Volt) asking the cabinet to ban generative AI use by pupils in primary education has majority support, including CDA, D66, JA21 and several smaller parties. It asks the government to adjust the *Regieplan Digitalisering Funderend Onderwijs* and set clear rules for AI in classrooms. A second motion asks for proper, certified AI training for teachers. [Computable](https://computable.nl/2026/10/30/tweede-kamer-wil-verbod-op-chatgpt-en-ai-op-basisscholen)

**Why it matters.** It is the first clear political signal in the Netherlands that primary pupils should not use tools like ChatGPT, and it follows New York's pause (covered last week) and Norway's near-total restriction.

**Context.** State Secretary Tielen argues pupils should learn *about* AI and that AI should support, not replace, learning. Minister Letschert wants the sector councils to lead, with national guidance from the ministry. The article reports majority support but no formal vote count, and a motion is not a law.

**What to watch.** How the cabinet translates this into the Regieplan, and whether "generative AI" covers AI features built into learning tools or only open chatbots. For lesson platforms, the line between teacher-facing AI (allowed) and pupil-facing AI (banned in PO) will matter a lot.

### 2. Flanders raises the bar for the first stage of secondary school (Belgium, K-12)
**What happened.** On 26 October, VRT NWS reported that the Flemish government approved minister Zuhal Demir's proposal for new minimum goals (*minimumdoelen*) in secondary education. The 16 broad key competences are replaced by goals per subject, with more emphasis on knowledge. By the end of the first stage (years 1-2), every pupil must reach the minimum goals for Dutch and maths; the class council keeps the final say. [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/25/nieuwe-minimumdoelen-in-middelbaar-onderwijs/)

**Why it matters.** It changes what all Flemish lower-secondary teachers must teach and assess, and it aligns secondary school with the already reformed, knowledge-heavy primary goals.

**Context.** The goals start in September 2029, when the first pupils from the new primary goals arrive. There will be separate frameworks for the A and B streams and for special secondary education. An independent expert committee will write the subject goals. VRT says it is still unclear what the "group" requirement for other subjects means in practice, and the article contained no reactions from unions or school networks.

**What to watch.** Reactions from the school networks (Katholiek Onderwijs Vlaanderen, GO!), the drafting timeline, and how schools will prove that every pupil reaches the Dutch and maths goals. For edtech: demand for lessons and diagnostics mapped to the new per-subject goals, well before 2029.

### 3. England pays £5.8bn to clear council SEND deficits, but not the future ones (United Kingdom, K-12)
**What happened.** Tes reported (29 Oct) that the government has paid about £5.79bn to 129 councils through a high-needs stability grant, covering 90% of their SEND deficits up to the end of 2025-26. Councils must cover the remaining 10% (over £600m). Payments depend on approved SEND reform plans; twelve councils are still waiting. [Tes](https://www.tes.com/magazine/news/general/government-spends-ps58bn-send-deficits)

**Why it matters.** SEND deficits have been the biggest threat to council and school budgets in England. This write-off removes the old debt, but the OBR forecasts new deficits of £4.2bn in 2026-27 and £5.7bn in 2027-28, with "no commitment" from government on those.

**Context.** A reformed SEND system is planned from 2029, with reformed EHCPs, three tiers of support and inclusion bases in mainstream schools. Councils' groups are asking for a long-term funding plan, and Tes separately reports the DfE was warned it must deliver one.

**What to watch.** Whether a funding plan for 2026-28 follows, and how the shift to more support in mainstream classrooms lands with teachers. For lesson platforms: more pupils with additional needs in mainstream lessons means more demand for accessible, adaptable materials.

## Insight for the LessonUp team
This week sharpens a split LessonUp should get ahead of: Dutch MPs want generative AI out of pupils' hands in primary school (Top 3, item 1), while the Eedi/DeepMind trial in UK schools suggests AI tutoring helps in the moment but did not move test scores after 10 weeks. The safe space is teacher-side AI: AI that helps teachers prepare, while the lesson itself stays teacher-led. Worth preparing a clear one-pager for PO schools on which LessonUp features are pupil-facing and which are teacher-only, before school boards start asking. Content idea: a ready-made "learning about AI without using it" lesson for groups 7-8, which fits both the motion and Tielen's "learn about AI" line.
