# Education Radar - week 44, 2026-10-29

## Week in one glance
AI in schools dominated the debate: New York put a one-year pause on student AI use for schoolwork, which is now fuelling a Dutch discussion, while Dutch university colleges (hogescholen) were told to write their own AI rules. In the UK, the Budget's school-funding projections and the unresolved SEND funding plan set the tone. Belgium had a quiet week, with a strike at an Aalst primary school the main verifiable item.

*Note: search tools surfaced recent news unevenly this week. Coverage was built mainly from direct fetches of Tes, AOb, ScienceGuide, VRT, Inside Higher Ed and EdWeek; some items are dated by URL only.*

## Top 3

### 1. New York pauses AI for student schoolwork, and the Netherlands asks "what about us?" (Netherlands, K-12)
**What happened.** According to AOb's Onderwijsblad (24 Oct), New York has banned AI for schoolwork by students up to high-school age, bars teachers from using AI to grade, and in effect pauses AI in schools for a year. In the Netherlands the question is whether primary schools should follow; HvA lector Izaak Dekker called it sensible but admitted a ban is hard to enforce and partly symbolic. [Source](https://www.aob.nl/onderwijsblad/rubrieken/new-york-verbiedt-gebruik-van-ai-voor-schoolwerk-en-nederland-dan/)

**Why it matters.** It is a clear case of a large system choosing "test first, adopt later" for generative AI, landing as Dutch higher education is also pushed on AI rules.

**Context.** Dekker argues generic chatbots do what users ask, whereas education aims at independent thinking. This is one expert's view, not Dutch policy.

**What to watch.** Whether Dutch ministers or school boards pick this up, and how "schoolwork" is defined in practice. For teachers: clarity on what is allowed. For LessonUp: tools that keep pupils actively thinking and make AI use transparent are better placed than answer-generating ones.

### 2. Phone-free schools: Ofsted rarely mentions phones, despite the push (United Kingdom, K-12)
**What happened.** A Tes analysis (20 Oct) found only 17 of 1,010 Ofsted report cards from April to July (1.7%) mention pupil phone policies; all mentions were positive. Ofsted says it could judge behaviour and attendance below the expected standard if phones disrupt learning. Teacher Tapp polling from September found about 52-54% of secondary schools run an "out of sight all day" policy. [Tes](https://www.tes.com/magazine/news/general/what-do-ofsted-school-inspections-say-about-mobile-phones)

**Why it matters.** It shows the gap between national guidance and measurable enforcement in a debate that also drives the Dutch and Flemish phone-ban discussions.

**Context.** The government has said all English schools should be phone-free, but resists a statutory ban. The data covers a short window and early inspections under the new framework.

**What to watch.** Whether DfE's monitoring produces numbers, and whether inspections start citing phones more. For edtech: phone-free days put more weight on school-owned devices and teacher-led, shared-screen activities.

### 3. Flemish study: Gen Z is more media-literate than expected, but not equally for everyone (Belgium, media literacy)
**What happened.** A study by Arteveldehogeschool for Flemish media minister Cieltje Van Achter, surveying almost 4,500 Flemings aged 16-98, found 81% can identify AI-generated images and nearly two-thirds understand how algorithms work. Older and lower-educated people struggle more. [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/28/jongeren-mediawijzer-dan-gedacht/)

**Why it matters.** It counters the narrative of young people as helpless online, and supports targeted rather than blanket media-literacy teaching.

**Context.** Self-reported and tested skills can differ; I only read the summary, not the full report. Belgium had little other verifiable classroom news this week, so this is the strongest available Belgian item.

**What to watch.** Whether the findings feed into Flemish curriculum or eindtermen debates. For lesson platforms: demand for ready-made AI-image and algorithm literacy lessons, differentiated by level.

## Insight for the LessonUp team
The Ofsted phone data (item 2) and New York's AI pause (item 1) point the same way: schools are drawing hard lines around personal devices and generative AI, while still wanting digital learning. LessonUp's opportunity is to be the "teacher-led, shared-screen" option: worth testing a ready-made lesson series on spotting AI images and how algorithms work (building on the Flemish study), and a clear message for schools that LessonUp works in phone-free and AI-cautious settings. Product question to explore: can teachers show at a glance that a lesson needs no pupil phones and no generative AI? Do not claim this today without checking the product.
