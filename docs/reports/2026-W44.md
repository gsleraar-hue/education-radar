## Week in one glance
A Dartmouth provost resigned after a faculty review found undisclosed, heavy AI editing of his writing, a high-profile test of AI-disclosure norms. Meanwhile US federal higher-ed policy and campus finances remain turbulent.

## Top 3

### 1. Dartmouth provost resigns over undisclosed AI use (US, higher ed)
**What happened:** Provost Santiago Schnell resigned on 28 Oct after a faculty-led committee concluded he used AI to substantially alter his writing and fell short of academic-integrity standards. Records from Dartmouth's enterprise Claude showed heavy editing of a journal manuscript and of peer-review responses; the publisher required disclosure, which was not given. He maintains AI did not replace his judgment or authorship. The resignation came under two weeks before a planned no-confidence vote. ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/governance/executive-leadership/2026/10/28/dartmouth-provost-resigns-over-ai-use))

**Why it matters:** The issue was less AI use itself than non-disclosure and inconsistent public statements, the same line schools draw for students. It also shows institutional AI logs being used as evidence.

**Context:** AI detectors (Pangram) triggered the story, but the committee relied on usage logs, so detector accuracy is not the settled basis here. Reports of detector scores come via secondary coverage; treat them with caution.

**What to watch:** Disclosure policies at publishers and universities; whether others adopt log-based reviews. For teachers: a clear precedent for "declare how you used AI". For edtech: demand for transparent AI-use records and student-facing disclosure features.

### 2. "Techlash" and tech scepticism among educators and parents (US, K-12)
**What happened:** Two EdWeek Research Center pieces (28-29 Oct): newer educators are less likely than veterans to say tech helps students learn, and suburban/rural educators report more parent concern about screen time than urban ones. In the survey of 596 educators (fielded Feb-Mar 2026), 57% of urban educators said parents see tech levels as "just right", versus 40% suburban and 38% rural. ([Newer educators](https://www.edweek.org/technology/newer-educators-are-less-likely-to-say-tech-helps-students-learn/2026/10), [Techlash](https://www.edweek.org/technology/techlash-is-strongest-in-suburban-and-rural-schools/2026/10))

**Why it matters:** The next generation of teachers is more sceptical, and parents' stance varies by community.

**Context:** Large districts have restricted student tech and AI (New York, Los Angeles, per Sept coverage). The survey is modest in size and fielded months ago; it measures perceptions, not learning outcomes. One interviewee noted parents back hands-on digital making more than adaptive platforms.

**What to watch:** Procurement scrutiny. For lesson platforms: position tools as teacher-led and active (not passive screen time), and show evidence of learning impact.

### 3. US Education Secretary's "reckoning" speeches and a campus closure (US, higher ed)
**What happened:** On 28 Oct at Vanderbilt, Linda McMahon called for a return to free speech, merit standards and institutional neutrality, citing agreements with seven institutions. Separately, the University of Providence (Montana) announced closure for Aug 2027 after years of losses and enrollment falling from 957 to 430. ([McMahon](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/government/politics-elections/2026/10/29/mcmahon-institutional-decay-compelled-reckoning), [Providence](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/business/financial-health/2026/10/29/university-providence-close))

**Why it matters:** Federal pressure and financial fragility are reshaping US higher education; spillover affects international research collaboration and funding in Europe.

**Context:** Preliminary enrollment data this week reportedly shows slower growth (Inside Higher Ed headline only; details not verified). The two stories are not causally linked.

**What to watch:** More small-college closures; European responses to US research-funding politics. Limited direct impact on K-12 lesson platforms.

## Education tip for the LessonUp team
**Make AI use visible: "show your process" checkpoints.** The Dartmouth case shows that disclosure, not use, is the issue. Research on formative assessment (Black & Wiliam, 1998) supports frequent low-stakes checks that reveal student thinking. Combine them: have students draft first, then use AI, then reflect. *In an interactive lesson:* slide 1, open question "Write your own answer first"; slide 2, show an AI-generated answer; slide 3, a multiple-choice "What did the AI get wrong or leave out?"; slide 4, a short "How I used AI" reflection poll. The teacher sees thinking before and after, and students practise honest declaration.
