# Education Radar - week 46, 2026-11-11
*Last refreshed: 2026-11-11 07:40 (local time)*

## Week in one glance
Screens dominated the week in England: ministers told schools to make phones fully inaccessible all day (from September 2027) and, a day later, proposed safety certification for edtech, after a minister warned of a "Wild West" of products and criticised gamified apps. In French-speaking Belgium, the first-ever CLÉ test showed that only about half of Year 4 pupils have a firm grip on reading comprehension and vocabulary. The Netherlands had a quieter week for schools, with debate on tutoring, special-school capacity and AI in higher education rather than big new policy.

## Top 3

### 1. England moves from "not seen, not heard" to truly phone-free schools (United Kingdom, K-12)
**What happened.** On 9-10 November the Department for Education announced strengthened mobile phone guidance: schools should go beyond policies where phones stay silent in bags and adopt whole-school strategies that keep phones "out of use and inaccessible throughout the school day", for example lockable pouches, collection at the gate, secure storage or a ban on site. According to the BBC, the changes take effect from September 2027; the existing statutory guidance has applied since June. [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/strengthened-guidance-to-take-the-next-leap-to-phone-free-schools), [BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c51kdk422w8jo)

**Why it matters.** The DfE says only 16% of secondary pupils report that phone rules are followed all the time. This is the clearest statement yet from a European government that the school day should be smartphone-free.

**Context.** Head teachers' unions (NAHT, ASCL) support the aim but warn that pouches or lockers for every pupil cost a lot of money and that no extra funding has been promised. One Manchester school spent about £20,000. The Netherlands is tightening its own phone agreement from 2027 (see last week's Background).

**What to watch.** Funding, and how schools enforce the rules. For lesson platforms, a phone-free day means any digital work runs on school-managed laptops or tablets, so lessons must work well on shared devices and when switching between screen and paper.

### 2. First CLÉ test: Wallonia-Brussels pupils struggle with reading comprehension and vocabulary (Belgium, K-12)
**What happened.** Education minister Valérie Glatigny (MR) presented the results of the first CLÉ test to the parliament of the Fédération Wallonie-Bruxelles on 9 November. The test was taken by 49,329 Year 4 primary pupils between 15 and 30 September. Maths: 61% pass rate overall, with weak spots in area and perimeter, problem solving and fractions. French: reading fluency was good (97.5 words per minute on average) and writing reached 62.8%, but reading comprehension was only 54% and vocabulary 49%. [La Libre Belgique](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/enseignement/2026/11/09/le-test-cle-met-en-lumiere-differentes-lacunes-des-eleves-de-4e-primaire-en-francais-et-mathematiques-MPACQHJDCVGWTPHEUDIUNXUJHE/)

**Why it matters.** It is a new system-wide diagnostic in the middle of primary school, giving French-speaking Belgium hard data on skills while there is still time to catch up. The gap between fast reading and weak comprehension matches what PISA and Flemish tests show.

**Context.** The minister says the results will be used to improve personalised support for pupils. This comes during a tense period: unions oppose a €500 million savings package, have planned demonstrations on 17 and 23 November, and only resumed talks with the government on 10 November. [La Libre](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/enseignement/2026/11/10/fwb-syndicats-des-enseignants-et-gouvernement-renouent-les-fils-du-dialogue-social-7MAJ4WSVQJCJNK7ZUANC3I7R74/) It is not yet clear what support schools will actually get.

**What to watch.** Results by school and by group, and the remediation plan. For edtech: a clear need for materials on vocabulary, comprehension and fractions in Year 4-5.

### 3. England proposes safety certification for edtech, including AI tutors and personalised learning tools (United Kingdom, edtech)
**What happened.** On 10 November the DfE opened a consultation on a certification scheme for education technology. Four product types are in scope: filtering systems, products doing "high-risk" processing of children's data (such as AI tutors, SEND platforms and MIS), pupil-facing generative AI tools, and pupil-facing personalised learning tools. Products would need to "minimise the risk of emotional or developmental harm to children". The scheme does not judge educational effectiveness, and Tes reports it would be "technically non-mandatory". [Tes](https://www.tes.com/magazine/news/general/dfe-sets-out-edtech-safety-standards)

**Why it matters.** It is the first attempt by a large European school system to set safety rules for edtech products themselves, rather than leaving schools to check every tool.

**Context.** A day earlier, minister Paul Waugh told MPs that schools risk a "Wild West" of edtech and called gamification "propaganda" to increase screen time. MPs raised concerns about TT Rockstars, which is now switching on curfews and daily time limits by default. [Tes](https://www.tes.com/magazine/news/general/schools-want-assurance-on-edtech-safety), [BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c64g1vyxq4j5o) The government is also drafting screen-time guidelines for 5-16-year-olds.

**What to watch.** The final criteria, whether certification becomes a buying condition for schools in practice, and whether other countries copy the model. For lesson platforms: design choices such as points, leaderboards and time-on-task are now being questioned by policymakers.

## Insight for the LessonUp team
England has now put gamification itself on the political agenda: a minister called it "propaganda" for screen time, TT Rockstars switched leaderboards off by default, and the DfE's proposed certification asks whether pupil-facing tools could cause "emotional or developmental harm" (Top 3, item 3). Expect Dutch and Flemish schools to ask the same questions soon, especially with phone bans already in place. Worth preparing: a clear, honest answer to "how does LessonUp use points, leaderboards and screen time?", plus a check of which game elements are on by default and whether teachers can switch them off. Marketing idea: position LessonUp as teacher-led, short-burst classroom use, not open-ended screen time.
